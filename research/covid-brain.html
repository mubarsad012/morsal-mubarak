Overview

Some people continue to have neurological and cognitive symptoms long after a COVID-19 infection, a form of Long COVID that researchers call neuroPASC (neurological post-acute sequelae of COVID-19). The multicenter COVID-BRAIN study used proton magnetic resonance spectroscopy (1H-MRS), a noninvasive MRI-based method that measures chemicals involved in brain metabolism, to examine the thalamus, the brain's central relay hub.

The study analyzed 176 participants (102 with neuroPASC and 74 controls); those with neuroPASC were studied on average about two years after infection. People with neuroPASC had lower thalamic glutathione, an antioxidant, and lower total creatine, an energy buffer, than controls, whether or not they had been hospitalized during the initial infection. In people with neuroPASC, higher creatine went along with better performance on a cognitive test (Stroop Color-Word). Lower myo-inositol tracked worse fatigue and depression scores in both groups. These are associations, not proof of cause and effect, and they need confirmation in further research.

Why this study

  • Long COVID affects roughly 1 in 16 U.S. adults (a CDC figure cited on the poster). Early-pandemic estimates were higher, and many people need ongoing clinical management.
  • Most Long COVID studies follow people to about 1 year after infection. This study looked at an average of 2 years after the initial infection.
  • The multi-site COVID-BRAIN Advanced Imaging Network studies the long-term effects of SARS-CoV-2 infection on the brain, neurological health, cognition, and quality of life. Participating sites: UMN, Mayo Clinic, MGH, JHU, and Houston Methodist.

What is neuroPASC?

Neurological post-acute sequelae of COVID-19: persistent neurological and cognitive symptoms after a confirmed SARS-CoV-2 infection.

On the poster, that means a PCR-, antigen- or antibody-confirmed infection, with neurological symptoms that began within 6 months and were still present at the study visit (COVID-Neuro checklist).

What is 1H-MRS?

Proton magnetic resonance spectroscopy, an MRI-based technique that measures brain metabolite concentrations.

Instead of a picture, each scan gives a spectrum of peaks from different chemicals. Software (LCModel) fits the spectrum to estimate how much of each chemical is present.

Why the thalamus?

The thalamus is the brain's central relay and integrative hub, sitting deep in the middle of the brain.

The team measured spectra from both sides of the thalamus together (a bilateral thalamic voxel).

The poster suggests this may reflect an interaction between persistent systemic immune perturbation and altered cerebral redox homeostasis: lasting immune changes in the body alongside a shifted antioxidant balance in the brain.

Methods

Recruit

2,057 people expressed interest, 434 were screened by phone, and 176 were analyzed: 102 with neuroPASC and 74 controls.

Assess

Questionnaires on fatigue, anxiety, sleep and depression; cognitive tests; blood markers of inflammation.

Scan

3T Siemens Prisma MRI, 32-channel head coil. 1H-MRS spectra from the bilateral thalamus and the pons.

Quantify

LCModel fitting. A metabolite was kept if its cohort-average CRLB was below 20%, which left seven in the thalamus.

Analyze

ANCOVA for group and subgroup differences; partial Spearman correlations; FDR values for multiple comparisons.

Who took part

  • neuroPASC: PCR-, antigen- or antibody-confirmed infection; neurological symptoms began within 6 months and persisted at the study visit (COVID-Neuro checklist).
  • Controls: people without a symptomatic SARS-CoV-2 infection.
  • Key exclusions: pre-existing neurological or cognitive disorders, severe psychiatric disorder, prior ME/CFS; WHO ordinal 6–7 or invasive ventilation during the acute illness.

What was measured

  • Questionnaires: fatigue (MFIS), anxiety (GAD-7), sleep (PSQI), depression (PHQ-8).
  • Cognitive tests: MoCA Blind, Stroop, SDMT.
  • Blood markers: peripheral hsCRP, IL-6, TNF-α.
  • Brain chemistry: seven thalamic metabolites: Cr+PCr, GSH, Glu, Glu+Gln, Ins, NAA+NAAG, PCho+GPC.

How it was analyzed

  • Quality rule: a metabolite was included if its cohort-average CRLB (Cramér–Rao lower bound, a fitting-uncertainty estimate) across both groups was below 20%, with no per-participant filtering (Kreis, MRM 2016).
  • Group differences: ANCOVA adjusted for age, sex, and site; subgroup analyses compared non-hospitalized and hospitalized neuroPASC with controls.
  • Correlations: partial, age/sex-adjusted Spearman rank (rs) for cognitive, symptom and blood-marker associations.

What it means and its limits

The poster's conclusions, in plain language:

  1. About two years after infection, people with neuroPASC had lower thalamic glutathione (antioxidant defense) and lower total creatine (an energy buffer) than controls, whether or not they had been hospitalized during the acute infection.
  2. Thalamic chemistry tracked cognition. In neuroPASC, higher creatine went with better Stroop Color-Word performance; controls showed no such relationship.
  3. In the hospitalized subgroup, lower glutathione went with higher blood hs-CRP and a higher intracellular volume fraction (ICVF). The authors see this as consistent with a hypothesized inflammation-linked metabolic and microstructural process, such as cellular swelling or glial change. The hs-CRP association was nominal (FDR = 0.425).
  4. Lower myo-inositol tracked greater symptom burden, the opposite direction from classic gliosis. The authors read this as possible glial dysfunction, similar to reports in chronic fatigue syndrome and Long COVID.
  5. The authors suggest that cerebral energy metabolism and antioxidant defense are measurable and potentially targetable processes in chronic neuroPASC. This needs further study.

Read these results with care.

  • These are associations, not proof of cause and effect, and they need confirmation in further research.
  • Two results did not hold up after correcting for multiple comparisons: glutathione × MoCA Blind (FDR = 0.163) and glutathione × hsCRP (FDR = 0.425), both in the hospitalized subgroup.
  • Subgroup results rest on fewer people: 23 hospitalized neuroPASC participants had thalamic spectra (poster Table 1).
  • Myo-inositol levels themselves did not differ significantly between groups, and their link with fatigue and depression appeared in controls as well as in neuroPASC.
  • Nothing here is a diagnostic test or treatment advice.
