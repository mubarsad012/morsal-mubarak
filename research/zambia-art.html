Antiretroviral Therapy (ART) Shortages and Adolescent HIV in Zambia

Chiara Cimarusti and Morsal Mubarak

Most research on why adolescents in Zambia go untreated for HIV points to stigma, cost, and awareness. We asked a different question: what if the supply side matters too — how many clinics exist, how many health workers staff them, and whether the medicine is on the shelf? Using Zambia Demographic and Health Survey trend data from 2018 to 2024 and a review of the published literature, we trace how one shortage multiplies into the next.

Overview

HIV targets the immune system by destroying CD4 T-cells, which over time leaves a person open to infections a healthy immune system would shrug off. Antiretroviral therapy (ART) improves long-term survival and reduces onward transmission — but only for people who can actually get it and stay on it. In Zambia, adolescents aged 15 to 19 remain a key at-risk group.

The literature on why they go untreated is dominated by stigma, cost, and awareness. Our paper examines whether a shortage of medical resources — health facilities, health workers, and ART supply itself — is also part of the picture. We find a positive relationship: regions with fewer facilities and less access to ART show lower proportions of treated HIV and higher proportions of untreated HIV. More importantly, the shortages are not independent. The paper describes them as having a multiplicative effect: each one makes the next one worse.

Too few health facilities

“Insufficient health facilities increases distance to care”

Paper, section V (Findings), p. 5

Read end to end, the chain is the paper's central claim: a facility shortage is not just a facility shortage. It becomes a distance problem, then a money problem, and finally a decision an adolescent makes about whether treatment is worth the trip. Meanwhile the staffing shortage degrades the care they get if they do arrive, and the stigma loop keeps them from testing in the first place. Fixing any single link leaves the others intact — which is why the paper proposes four interventions rather than one.

What the literature shows

Seven sources make up the evidence base. Each line below is the finding as our paper reports it.

  • Dube et al., 2010

    Mobile ART services in rural Zambia helped overcome challenges associated with the lack of health facilities; when barriers are reduced, proportions of adolescents on ART increase.

    10.2471/BLT.09.063982
  • Fox et al., 2010

    Costs, stigma and awareness were the major reasons for delayed initiation of ART in urban and rural areas of Zambia.

    10.1186/1758-2652-13-8
  • Katirayi et al., 2021

    Fear of stigma and myths surrounding HIV discouraged youth from getting tested and starting treatment.

    10.1186/s12889-021-10391-x
  • Miti et al., 2020

    Drug resistance was a major concern in the adolescent ART-experienced population in Ndola, Zambia.

    10.1371/journal.pone.0236156
  • Mwamba et al., 2022

    Newly diagnosed people usually wait to start treatment because of preparedness, fear and health system barriers.

    10.1080/07853890.2022.2051069
  • Nichols et al., 2021

    Community-based ART distribution was cost-effective and produced better adherence and health outcomes.

    10.1097/QAD.0000000000002737
  • Wilson, 2016

    Listed in the reference list; not discussed in the body text.

    10.1016/j.ehb.2016.02.003

What this body of work leaves out is the gap we aimed at: these studies explain delayed initiation and poor adherence, but not the effect of the shortage of medical resources itself on HIV-positive older adolescents.

Four proposed interventions

Each one targets a different link in the chain, and the paper notes they may be used in combination to achieve better results.

1

Increasing mobile ART clinics

Enhancing the number of mobile clinics providing ART may enhance ART access among adolescents.

Targets: steps 1–2, facilities and distance. Cited evidence: Dube et al., 2010.

2

Intensifying school-based HIV/AIDS education

Knowledge and information on HIV/AIDS provided through education in schools can help reduce HIV/AIDS-related stigma and enhance HIV testing among adolescents.

Targets: the stigma loop. Cited evidence: Katirayi et al., 2021.

3

Implementation of community-based ART

Provision of ART from community-based facilities has the potential of enhancing ART access and adherence.

Targets: steps 2–4, distance, cost and follow-through. Cited evidence: Nichols et al., 2021.

4

Increasing funding for health facility infrastructure

Funding for the construction of clinics, hiring of health care staff, and the supply of ART may help reduce shortages in resources and infrastructure.

Targets: step 1 and the health-worker branch. Cited evidence: none cited in the paper.

The discussion makes an argument that is easy to miss: addressing the supply shortage may also soften the stigma problem. More ART uptake normalizes treatment within a community, so a supply fix can have a social effect that no awareness campaign on its own produces.
