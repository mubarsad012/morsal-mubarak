Academic Pressure & Mental Health in South Asian American High-School Students

Morsal Mubarak, Anzalah Qadir, Harleen Mangat, Madeleine Ramirez, Mahnoor Raza, Paris Ly, Sankirna Sridhar.

We surveyed South Asian and South-East Asian high-school students about academic pressure, how they cope with it, and whether they ever ask for help. The pattern that came back is uncomfortable: most respondents report stress-related mental-health concerns regularly, students name themselves more often than anyone else as the source of their pressure, and the vast majority have never sought professional help — because they do not think they need it.

Overview

Academic pressure is not automatically harmful — for plenty of students it is the thing that gets them to study. The question is where it tips over, and what happens when it does. For students in South Asian and South-East Asian families, academic achievement is often tied to family expectations, cultural values, and plans for life after high school, which can make the pressure harder to name as a problem.

We surveyed high-school students across several cities and towns with an anonymous questionnaire mixing rating scales and multiple-choice questions, covering academic stressors, coping strategies, motivation, and help-seeking. Keeping it anonymous was deliberate: these are questions students have an obvious reason to answer less than honestly if their name is attached.

How to read these charts. The paper never states a sample size, so every total below is described as “responses shown on the chart” — the sum of the labels printed on that chart, nothing more. Two questions let students pick more than one answer, which is why their totals run higher than the others; those are flagged on the charts. Treat all of this as a small, self-selected student survey, not a population estimate.

What we read into it

The pressure is internalized

Students acknowledge that cultural and family expectations shape their lives, but they overwhelmingly name fear of failure and worry about their future — not explicit pressure from family — as the stressor they feel. The pressure chart says the same thing numerically: 22 responses for pressure from yourself against 15 for pressure from parents.

That matters for anyone trying to help. Expectations do not have to be stated out loud to be felt, so interventions aimed only at parents will miss most of what students are carrying.

Stress is being normalized, not treated

93% of respondents report stress-related mental-health concerns regularly, yet 89% have never sought professional help because they felt they did not need it. Read together, those two numbers suggest students are not refusing help so much as failing to recognize that what they are experiencing qualifies.

Published work gives some context for the gap: a systematic review found stigma identified by around 73% of people surveyed as a barrier to seeking mental-health help (Clement et al., 2015). That figure is from the literature we cite, not from our survey.

The survey also found the other edge of this. Many students described pressure as a motivator — something that pushed them to keep their grades up, chase more opportunities, and build a stronger work ethic. The paper's framing is that academic pressure works in a double-edged way, and our results do not let us pick one side of it.

What we recommend

Six recommendations follow from the results, and the first one follows directly from the coping chart: if students go to friends first, build on that instead of around it.

1

Establish peer support programs

Structured groups where students talk through academic stressors and coping strategies, meeting regularly to break the sense of isolation.

2

Implement student mentorship programs

Pair younger students with older ones who have already navigated the same pressure and can normalize it while offering practical advice.

3

Integrate mental health education

Put mental-health literacy in the curriculum so students can recognize the warning signs of long-term stress, anxiety and burnout.

4

Work towards destigmatization

Awareness campaigns, guest speakers, workshops and student-led initiatives that make talking about mental health ordinary.

5

Improve access to mental health services

Make counselors visible and easy to reach, and make sure students actually know what is on offer.

6

Promote healthy notions of success

Help students define success beyond grades — personal growth, resilience, well-being and balance — to curb unhealthy perfectionism.
